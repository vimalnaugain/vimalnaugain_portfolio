Case study

Making Airtel bills easier to understand

Project
Airtel Bill Simplification | FY26
Role
Content Designer
Team
1 DGM Content Design, 2 Lead UX Designers, 1 Product Manager
Success measurement
Jan–Mar 2027
  1. 01Understanding the problem
  2. 02Auditing the existing bill
  3. 03Defining the goal
  4. 04Setting the content direction
  5. 05Restructuring the bill
  6. 06Designing for real billing situations
  7. 07Looking beyond copy
  8. 08Measuring the impact
  9. 09What I took away

A bill should tell customers what they’re paying, why they’re paying it and what they need to do next.

But Airtel’s existing billing experience didn’t always make this clear. Customers could see the charges and amounts, but often needed customer care to understand why their bill had changed.

I worked on improving how billing information was organised and explained across the app. The goal was to help customers understand their bills, make sense of their spending and resolve more questions on their own.

01

Understanding the problem

I started by looking at assisted customer-care contacts to understand how much of the problem was related to billing.

The data showed a clear opportunity:

  • 40%of assisted customer-care contacts were about billing.
  • 60%of billing-related contacts were about late fees.
  • 92%of billing contacts came from just three scenarios: late fees, mid-cycle plan changes and international roaming.

The data helped us move beyond general assumptions and focus on the problems affecting customers the most.

What customers were asking

ScenarioCustomer questionShare of billing contacts
Late feesWhy is there an extra charge on my bill?60%
Mid-cycle plan changesWhy am I paying for two plans?18%
International roamingRoaming charges? I was in India.14%

The opportunity was clear: Help customers understand these charges before they had to contact customer care.

02

Auditing the existing bill

I went through the existing bill line by line, looking at its labels, amounts, colours, information structure and empty states.

Four key problems emerged:

  1. 1
    Charges without explanations

    The bill showed amounts but didn’t always explain why the total had changed.

  2. 2
    Surprises a month later

    Late fees and adjustments appeared in the following bill, disconnected from the event that caused them.

  3. 3
    System-led language

    Labels such as “Other usage charges” described internal billing categories rather than what customers had actually purchased.

  4. 4
    Mixed signals

    Green was used for items that didn’t represent savings, while empty sections occupied space without adding useful information.

The key insight: customers didn’t necessarily need more information. They needed the existing information to be organised and explained better.

03

Defining the goal

We wanted customers to find answers in the app before reaching customer care.

The existing experience had three layers:

  1. AppDidn’t explain all charges clearly.
  2. ChatPartly resolved customer questions.
  3. Customer careRequired manual intervention to resolve them.

Our focus was to address the first point of confusion: the bill itself.

Success target20% fewer billing-related customer-care calls in Jan–Mar 2027.

04

Setting the content direction

Before rewriting the content, I looked at how other products explained money.

We studied 15+ apps, including Amazon, Uber, Zomato and Airbnb, to understand how they presented bills, receipts, charges and payment information.

Three principles stood out:

  1. 01Highlight the key amount

    The total payable should be easy to find. Supporting details should follow.

  2. 02Disclose everything upfront

    Charges, late fees, reversals and adjustments should be visible before customers have to ask about them.

  3. 03Make colour meaningful

    Red should flag charges and delays. Green should be reserved for actual savings, waivers and benefits.

These principles became the foundation for the changes that followed.

05

Restructuring the bill

The old bill presented charges as a long list, with amounts hidden under broad labels such as “Other usage charges”.

I reorganised the information into six categories based on what customers were actually being charged for:

  1. Plan rentalPostpaid and Wi-Fi rental charges.
  2. PenaltiesEMI bounce, cheque bounce and other penalty charges.
  3. International roamingRoaming-related charges.
  4. One-time chargesData boosters, SIM or router replacements and activation charges.
  5. Repeat chargesRecurring add-ons such as caller tunes and data boosters.
  6. SubscriptionsServices such as Amazon, Netflix and Google One.

This gave every charge a clear place in the bill. It also helped separate recurring and one-time charges, show taxes clearly and make the total payable easier to understand.

06

Designing for real billing situations

A bill isn’t a single moment. Customers interact with it before making a payment, when a charge appears, when their plan changes or when they need an explanation.

Based on the customer-care data, I worked across four key scenarios.

Scenario 1

Due and overdue bills

The problem

Customers often discovered late fees only after they appeared in their bills. They needed to understand the possible charge before the due date and its impact after payment became overdue.

What I changed

  • Reworked the NBA (Next Best Action) message to highlight the due date and upcoming late fee.
  • Made the overdue amount and late fee easier to identify.
  • Explained late-fee waivers and payment actions more clearly.
  • Improved the bill breakup to show the fee, waiver and final payable amount separately.

The aim was to reduce surprises and make the next action clear.

Scenario 2

First bill and estimated charges

The problem

New customers had to wait for their first bill to understand what they would pay. Their first billing cycle could cover only part of a month, making the final amount difficult to anticipate.

What I changed

I introduced a first-bill notification that explained:

  • When the first bill would arrive.
  • The estimated amount.
  • The billing period covered by the estimate.
  • That additional purchases could change the final amount.

I also moved the notification to the Postpaid category page, where customers manage their connection.

This helped customers understand what to expect before their first bill arrived.

Scenario 3

International roaming

The problem

Customers travelling near international borders could incur roaming charges even when they hadn’t left India.

What I changed

I worked on communication across three stages:

  1. PreventPrompt customers to turn off international roaming before charges begin.
  2. CheckExplain the waiver verification process and what was being checked.
  3. ResolveClearly communicate the charges removed and why.

The aim was to keep customers informed throughout the process rather than leave them guessing.

Scenario 4

Mid-cycle plan changes

The problem

When customers changed plans during a billing cycle, they could see charges for both plans. Without an explanation, this could look like duplicate billing.

What I changed

I proposed a plan rental breakdown showing:

  • Each plan active during the billing cycle.
  • The dates each plan was active.
  • Its share of the total rental.

This made the calculation easier to follow and explained why both plans appeared on the bill.

07

Looking beyond copy

One important finding came from the late-fee scenario.

The late fee was being charged in the following month’s bill rather than when the payment became overdue. This disconnected the charge from the event that caused it.

Better copy alone couldn’t fully solve this.

We proposed two improvements:

Change the product behaviour

Charge the late fee when it becomes due, and show it as already settled in the next month’s invoice.

Improve the content structure

Make charges, adjustments, taxes and payment history easier to find and understand.

This was an important part of my work: recognising when content could solve the problem and when the underlying product experience needed to change.
08

Measuring the impact

The redesigned experience aims to help customers understand their bills, face fewer surprises and resolve more questions independently in the app.

20%reduction in billing-related customer-care calls
Success target
20% reduction in billing-related customer-care calls.
Measurement period
Jan–Mar 2027
Status
Awaiting measurement
09

What I took away

  1. Let data pick the scenarios.

    Customer-care questions helped identify where content interventions mattered most.

  2. Structure before sentences.

    The six categories made later communication shorter and clearer because every charge already had a place.

  3. Not every problem is a copy problem.

    The late-fee issue needed a change to when the charge was applied, not just a better explanation.

Case study

Making Airtel’s next best action more useful

Project
Airtel Next Best Action (NBA) | 2025–26
Role
Content Designer — Content logic, information hierarchy, clip tags and copy
Team
1 Content Design Lead, 1 Product Manager, 1 Designer
Product
Airtel app home page
  1. 01Understanding the problem
  2. 02Phase 1
  3. 03Making priority visible with clip tags
  4. 04Defining the content direction
  5. 05Restructuring the NBA experience
  6. 06Relevant recommendations
  7. 07Looking beyond the card
  8. 08Measuring the impact
  9. 09My contribution
  10. 10What I took away

Helping users understand what needs their attention, why it matters and what they should do next.

As the Content Designer, I contributed to both the content and the logic behind the NBA experience.

01

Understanding the problem

Users had actions to take. They didn’t always know which one mattered first.

Before NBA, the Airtel app home page showed a simple service card with the number of services a user had and a status message such as “No action pending” or “1 pending action”.

But when something needed attention, the card didn’t explain what the action was, which connection it belonged to or why it mattered.

This became harder for users managing multiple Airtel connections, such as prepaid, postpaid and several Wi-Fi accounts. They had to find the right connection, open its journey and then figure out what needed to be done.

The experience didn’t answer three basic questions:

  1. 1
    What needs my attention?
  2. 2
    Which connection is it for?
  3. 3
    What should I do now?

The old journey

  1. Home page
  2. Generic service card
  3. Find the connection
  4. Open the right journey
  5. Take action

The opportunity was to bring the most relevant action to the home page and give users enough information to act without having to search for it.

02

Phase 1

Putting an action on the home page was only the first step.

The first version of NBA brought key actions to the home page. Users could recharge when a prepaid plan was about to expire, pay a postpaid or Wi-Fi bill, and explore available packs.

It made important actions easier to find, but the experience was still largely product-driven. The messages focused on what Airtel wanted users to do rather than what was happening in their lives.

Three problems stood out:

  1. 1
    Generic information

    Messages told users that something was due, but didn’t provide enough context to understand the situation.

  2. 2
    Weak priority

    Different actions looked equally important, even when one needed attention sooner than another.

  3. 3
    Product-led language

    Messages such as “Recharge now”, “Pay your bill” and “Explore packs” focused on the action without explaining why it mattered to the user.

Phase 1 proved that NBA could bring actions closer to users. The next step was to make those actions easier to understand and prioritise.

03

Making priority visible with clip tags

Helping users scan before they read.

When multiple NBA cards appeared together, users needed a quick way to understand their status and urgency.

I created the content approach for clip tags: small labels placed on NBA cards to communicate the state of an action at a glance.

Clip tagPurposeFor cases
Payment StatusTracks the progress and outcome of a payment transaction.Pending, In Progress, Paid, Failed, Expired (in case of UPI request)
Pack StatusTracks the validity and lifecycle of a recharge pack or bundle.Active, Nearing Expiry, Expired, Renewed
Bill StatusTracks the billing state and payment requirement of a service.Unbilled, Bill Generated, Due Soon, Overdue, Paid
Plan StatusTracks the state of a customer’s subscribed plan or service.Active, Suspended, Disconnected, Pending Activation
Request StatusTracks the progress of a service request or support ticket.Raised, In Progress, Delayed, On Hold, Resolved, Closed

Claim BenefitHighlights benefits available to the user at no additional cost.Claim benefit, complimentary subscription, free data, loyalty reward
Special OfferHighlights upsell offers intended to drive engagement.Upgrade offer, recharge offer, bonus data offer, personalized discount
04

Defining the content direction

What do other products do to help people decide what to do next?

We looked at 15+ products, including:

  • Amazon Pay
  • CRED
  • Truebill
  • Amazon
  • Google
  • Gmail
  • Venmo
  • Wealthfront
  • Uniqlo
  • ChatGPT
  • TikTok
  • Greenlight
  • Crunch

We looked beyond the words to understand how these products:

  • Made urgent actions stand out.
  • Presented important information before asking users to act.
  • Explained why an action mattered.
  • Handled multiple recommendations on the same screen.

One pattern stood out. The strongest experiences didn’t simply tell people what to do. They helped them understand:

  1. This is happening
  2. This is why it matters
  3. This is what you can do

I used this as the foundation for the new NBA content approach.

05

Restructuring the NBA experience

From a recommendation card to a decision system.

The new experience needed to respond to a user’s current situation rather than show the same type of message for every action.

I mapped 100+ user states across bills, recharges, service requests, roaming, refunds and other Airtel services.

This helped establish what the NBA should communicate in each situation and how the message and visual treatment should change.

The four decisions that shaped the new system:

  1. 1
    Start with the user’s state

    Understand what is happening with the user’s connection before deciding what the card should say.

  2. 2
    Define what matters most

    Work with product to decide which action should appear first. For example, a plan expiring today should take priority over a bill due in three days.

  3. 3
    Build the content around the situation

    Every card follows a consistent structure.

  4. 4
    Design for multiple connections

    Show clear identifiers, such as a mobile number or DSL ID, so users can tell which connection an action belongs to without opening every card.

Every card follows a consistent structure

  1. SituationWhat is happening?
  2. ContextWhat does the user need to know?
  3. ConsequenceWhy does it matter?
  4. ActionWhat can the user do?
06

Relevant recommendations

NBA also had the potential to recommend products and offers. But showing an offer while a user had an urgent service action could make the experience feel more interested in selling than helping.

What I changed

We used the same priority logic to decide when recommendations could appear. The order was:

  1. Immediate actionFor example, a plan expiring today.
  2. Important and upcoming actionFor example, a bill due in three days.
  3. Relevant recommendationFor example, a credit card or fixed deposit.

The idea was simple: address the user’s immediate need first. Show a recommendation only when it is relevant and doesn’t compete with something more important.

07

Looking beyond the card

NBA should say what matters to the user, not what Airtel wants.

The experience evolved through three stages.

  1. Original service cardShowed the number of services and pending actions, but gave users no clarity on what needed their attention.
  2. Phase 1 NBABrought actions to the home page, but focused on telling users what to do rather than explaining their situation.
  3. Evolved NBAMade the user’s situation, urgency and next step clear, helping them understand what mattered most.
The larger change was not just better copy or a redesigned card. It was a clearer system for deciding what users should know, what they should do and why they should do it now.
08

Measuring the impact

The redesigned NBA experience focused on showing users more relevant actions, making them easier to understand and helping them reach the right journey.

What we achieved so far August 2026 data

  • ▲Increase18%in recharges completed before pack expiry
  • ▲Increase21%in bill payments
  • ▲Increase3%in new acquisitions through NBA cards
09

My contribution

As the Content Designer, I contributed to both the content and the logic behind the NBA experience.

  • Defined the content logic for different user states.
  • Mapped 100+ user states across Airtel services.
  • Structured the information hierarchy for NBA cards.
  • Decided what information should appear in the headline.
  • Created the content approach for clip tags.
  • Reworked NBA copy across different scenarios.
  • Defined priority-led communication with product and design.
  • Helped expand NBA beyond basic service reminders.

The NBA logic decides what users need to do next. Content design makes sure they understand why.

10

What I took away

  1. Relevance comes before recommendations.

    An action is useful only when it matters to the user’s current situation.

  2. Priority is part of content design.

    What appears first can be just as important as the words used to explain it.

  3. Good content gives people a reason to act.

    A clear instruction is useful. An instruction with the right context helps people make a decision.

Case study

Making international travel hassle-free with Travel Mode

A dedicated experience that made it easier for Airtel users to prepare for international travel, manage their roaming packs and get help while abroad.

Project
Airtel Travel Mode
Role
Content Designer
Product
Airtel Thanks App
  1. 01The impact
  2. 02Understanding the problem
  3. 03Introducing Travel Mode
  4. 04Designing for different user scenarios
  5. 05Making support easier to access
  6. 06What I took away
01

The impact

Travel Mode helped reduce monthly roaming-related customer care calls by 4% and pack latching issues by 10%.

  • ▼Reduction4%in monthly roaming-related customer care calls
  • ▼Reduction10%in pack latching issues

These improvements came from addressing two key problems: users struggled to find the information and support they needed while travelling, and some users weren’t configuring all the required device settings before departure.

We brought the essential roaming information, settings and support options together in one dedicated experience. We also proactively guided users through the settings they needed to check before travelling.

02

Understanding the problem

International roaming involves more than purchasing a pack. Users need to prepare their devices, understand what their pack includes, track their usage and know where to get help if something goes wrong.

However, this information and these actions weren’t easy to access in one place.

We identified two important gaps:

Difficulty managing roaming

Users needed an easier way to understand their pack, check usage and find relevant support while abroad. This could lead to avoidable queries to customer care.

Pack latching issues

Some users weren’t configuring all the required device settings before travelling. This could prevent their roaming pack from latching successfully.

We wanted to address both problems by making the experience easier to navigate and helping users take the right steps at the right time.

03

Introducing Travel Mode

We introduced a dedicated Travel Mode in the Airtel Thanks App, bringing together the information and actions users might need before and during their international trip.

Rather than making users search across different sections, Travel Mode gave them a single place to explore their roaming pack, check usage, configure device settings and access support.

The experience was designed around different user situations, because someone preparing for a trip has different needs from someone already travelling.
04

Designing for different user scenarios

We considered the different stages of a user’s international roaming journey and shaped the experience around them.

Scenario 1

Pack purchased, but travel hasn’t started

At this stage, users needed to know whether their pack was ready and what they needed to do before departure.

We showed the pack details, including data, calling minutes, SMS, validity and supported countries. We also included information about available add-ons.

One important part of this experience was proactively showing the device settings users needed to turn on before travelling:

  • Data Roaming
  • Network Selection Mode
  • VoLTE
  • Caller ID

This helped address a key reason behind pack latching issues: users reaching their destination without configuring all the necessary settings.

Scenario 2

Pack active and user is travelling

Once users reached their destination, their priority shifted from preparation to managing their roaming usage.

We showed their current location, pack activation status and validity, along with their remaining data, calling minutes and SMS.

Users could also purchase additional data and access support without having to search elsewhere in the app.

Scenario 3

Travelling internationally without an active pack

We also considered users who were already abroad but didn’t have an active international roaming pack.

For them, we highlighted the risk of high roaming charges and provided a direct option to purchase a roaming pack.

This helped users understand their situation and take action from the same experience.

Scenario 4

User has multiple packs

Users with multiple roaming packs needed to understand their available benefits and usage without getting confused between packs.

We showed their remaining data, calling minutes and SMS, along with an option to view their active packs.

This made it easier to keep track of their roaming benefits while travelling.

05

Making support easier to access

We included support options within Travel Mode so users could find help when they needed it abroad.

They could access Airtel’s toll-free support number or start a conversation on WhatsApp by simply sending a “Hi”.

By keeping support alongside pack information and usage, we made it easier for users to find the next step without navigating through other parts of the app.

06

What I took away

  1. Design for the stage, not just the product.

    Someone preparing for a trip needs different information from someone already abroad.

  2. Prevent the problem before it happens.

    Showing the device settings before departure addressed a key reason behind pack latching issues.

  3. Keep help next to the task.

    Placing support beside pack details and usage made the next step easier to find.

Case study

Gamifying the task experience for Wiom technicians

Project
Wiom technician task experience
Role
UX Writer
Team
2 Product Managers, 2 Designers
Company
Wiom
  1. 01Understanding the problem
  2. 02What technicians told us
  3. 03The idea
  4. 04How it works
  5. 05Measuring the impact
  6. 06What I took away

Technicians should be able to open the app and know which task comes first.

At Wiom, only 70% of technician tasks were completed on time. I wrote the content for a cricket-themed task experience that shows, at a glance, how much time is left on each ticket.

01

Understanding the problem

Only 70% of tasks were completed on time.

Technicians had deadlines for the tasks on their assigned work tickets. But the existing approach wasn’t motivating enough for them to act promptly.

We needed something scalable and easy to understand, regardless of a technician’s education level.

The opportunity was to help technicians see which task needed their attention first, without having to work it out.

02

What technicians told us

Technicians couldn’t tell which task came first.

I met technicians to understand what they struggled with in the field.

When the app was full of service tickets, they found it hard to tell which one to prioritise. We already showed when a ticket was created and the time to complete it. But this didn’t create the urgency to act early.

Who the user is

Colour first, with a few words to support it.

We liked the idea of a visual, colour-based solution with a little supporting text.

But a literal traffic light didn’t fit the story we wanted to tell or the behaviour we wanted to encourage. Gamification was already planned, so we asked technicians about their favourite game. The answer was cricket.

  2. Colour with a little text
  3. A cricket theme

We built the flows around these insights. The aim was an experience technicians could understand without adding to their cognitive load.

Users avoided Autopay on the Wiom app, and those who paid manually stayed for less time.

I wrote the content that showed users the long-term value of Autopay and put it front and centre on the Payment Method page.

01

Understanding the problem

Users avoided Autopay on the Wiom app.

Their reasons were their financial situations, distrust of online payments and limited digital experience.

Retention was lower for users who paid manually, so we needed a way to move more users onto Autopay.

Who the user is

02

Why convenience didn’t work

Autopay was new to low-income households, who weren’t sure they could rely on it.

We first pitched it as a way to make monthly payments easier. That did not work.

So we stopped selling convenience and showed the long-term value of Autopay instead.

03

The idea

Show what staying on Autopay earns.

  1. Up to ₹100 off at bill payment
  2. A monthly discount that grows
  3. Staying longer saves more

A monthly discount that increases the longer users stay signals that staying longer saves more. We also highlighted Autopay on the Payment Method page.

We explored two versions of the offer:

  1. Experiment 1Get flat ₹25 off each month.
  2. Experiment 2Get up to ₹100 off with incremental monthly discounts.

    A monthly discount that grows the longer users stay told them that staying longer saves more.

Improving the installation process for Wiom technicians

Wiom installation flow

2 Product Managers, 2 Designers

Wiom

  6. 06What I took away

Wiom needed all installation work done in the app, but technicians weren’t used to working in one.

I wrote the step-by-step copy and the micro-interactions that turned a tedious installation process into a guided, engaging one.

01

Understanding the problem

We wanted technicians to make fewer installation mistakes.

The goal was to connect the ISP portal to the app. Technicians have limited formal education, so the flow had to be simple, guide them one step at a time and teach good installation practices.

The old app process was one large form. Technicians could easily miss crucial items, such as ISP details, which led to delayed or failed installations.

02

What technicians told us

It took them days to understand the installation process.

I spoke informally with some technicians about their experience with the app. They said the process was transactional and offered no education.

Who the user is

Technicians who are not highly educated and are not very familiar with working in an app.

03

The idea

Split one big form into steps, and teach while technicians wait.

We reworked the legacy process into a new approach. The installation flow was split into four parts:

  1. 1
    Before the customer’s house

    Before technicians reach the customer.

  2. 2
    After arriving

    Once technicians are at the customer’s house.

  3. 3
    The installation process

    Includes an idle wait of 3–5 minutes for backend API checks.

  4. 4
    Post-installation

    After the installation is done.

The 3–5 minute wait became a chance to teach technicians to double-check their work.

04

How it works

One step per screen, with good practice shown along the way.

Before

One large form.

After

Multiple steps, with instructional copy guiding technicians to the next one.

  1. One step per screenReduces cognitive load.
  2. Learning while workingGood-practice education is shown during the process, including the waiting periods before the next step.
  3. Micro-interactionsMicro-interactions and gratifications improve the experience.

The early micro-interactions asked technicians whether they had reached the customer’s home, and asked them to take a selfie before starting. These aimed to engage technicians and make sure they looked presentable at the customer’s house.

05

Measuring the impact

The new flow improved compliance, reduced errors and increased technician engagement.

20%increase in installation accuracy
Compliance
Improved.
Errors

Engagement
Technician engagement increased.
06

What I took away

  1. Break big forms into steps.

    One large form made it easy to miss crucial items. One step per screen reduced cognitive load.

  2. Use waiting time to teach.

    The 3–5 minute idle wait for backend checks became a moment to teach technicians to double-check their work.

  3. Make the process engaging.

    Micro-interactions and gratifications turned a transactional process into one technicians could learn from.
